# Context and Motivation

**Date:** November 2026

---

## Where this came from

I have built applications in a two-sided matching context. Working on them, what
interested me was not a product feature but a category of modelling error: systems that
learn from interaction data they themselves produced.

This note exists to test whether that suspicion is correct rather than merely plausible.
No data from those applications appears here, and nothing below describes them.

## The originating observation

Put crudely, the thing I noticed:

> A system watches a user ignore messages, concludes something about that user, and
> then acts on the conclusion — by showing them to fewer people, or by warning
> others away from them.

The step that interests me is the inference. The observation is *behaviour*. The
conclusion is *a property of the person*. And between them sits a system that
largely determined the behaviour it just observed:

- She received a volume of messages that no one could answer, so "ignores chats" is
  partly a fact about the inbox, not about her.
- She was shown to relatively few people, so the sample the system is reasoning from
  is tiny and was not chosen at random.
- The messages she did receive were produced under the same ranking that decided who
  she saw, so the whole interaction pattern is endogenous.

None of that makes the signal useless. It makes it **uninterpretable without
knowing the exposure**, and the exposure is usually in a log nobody reads.

## Why it generalises past dating

Dating is just the sharpest instance. The same structure appears in every two-sided
marketplace:

| Domain | The "ignored" signal | Same trap? |
|---|---|---|
| Rides / delivery | Driver rejects trips | Yes — rejection rate partly reflects what was offered |
| Freelance marketplaces | Client never hires | Yes — proposals are responses to ranked exposure |
| Content platforms | Creator gets no engagement | Yes — reach was allocated, not earned |
| Support queues | Agent is slow | Yes — load is assigned by a router |
| Reputation systems | Seller has few reviews | Yes — few reviews means few sales means low ranking |

In each case a system allocates attention, observes the outcome, and then treats the
outcome as a property of the recipient. Dating is where it is easiest to see that
this is a *modelling* error rather than a business decision — because the artefact
being scored is a person.

## Why I think it is worth the time

Three reasons, in descending order of honesty.

1. **It is the interesting half of applied ML.** Fitting a model to a static table
   teaches generalisation and then stops. Everything genuinely hard about production
   ML is in the loop: data generated by the system, labels missing not at random,
   and a metric that changes what it measures.
2. **It is reusable as a method.** The audit question — *what is this policy doing to
   the people it never shows?* — applies to any system that allocates attention, not
   just matching. That generality is most of the reason the study is worth doing.
3. **It is a claim I can actually settle.** Not "build a matchmaker" — that fails for
   lack of data and everyone knows it. But "does an exposure-aware estimator recover
   preference where a naive one does not" is answerable in a fortnight, in simulation,
   by me.

## Why not just get real data

Because I cannot, and the claim does not need it.

- No public dataset of reciprocal matching includes the exposure context — who was
  shown, how often, in what position, under which policy version. Without those
  columns the interesting analysis is impossible. A dataset of *matches* tells you
  nothing about the people who were never shown.
- Real dating data is personal data. Scraping it is a straightforward wrong.
- Even with consent, a real dataset gives me outcomes, not counterfactuals. I could
  never answer "would she have replied if she had been shown more often".
- In simulation, **I know the true preferences** because I generated them. That makes
  a measurement possible — recovery error against ground truth — that has no analogue
  in observational data.

There is a risk in that, and doc 02 addresses it: a simulator built to make the method
look good proves nothing. Which is why the generating process is specified and frozen
before the estimator is written.

## What would make me abandon this

Stated up front, so that a bad result cannot be reframed as a good one:

- **If the naive estimator recovers the truth** (high rank correlation with known
  latent preference), then the mechanism I am describing is not operating in my model.
  The DGP is too weak — a parameter problem, not a finding. One redesign, then stop.
- **If the correction works only at implausible parameter values** — near-infinite
  data, or propensities that are known exactly when they never are in practice — then
  the result is a curiosity, and the honest write-up says so.
- **If it becomes product work.** The moment I start designing screens or scoring real
  people, this has stopped being research and should be closed.

## What I am not claiming

- Not that dating apps are broken.
- Not that any real group is treated unfairly. The model's populations are synthetic.
- Not that this is novel. The literature on exposure bias, inverse propensity scoring
  and performative prediction is mature; I am implementing and demonstrating known
  results in a domain where the failure is legible to a general audience.
